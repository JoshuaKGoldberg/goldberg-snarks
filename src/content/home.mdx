## Edge colorings with three colors

In the graphs on this site, two vertices are joined by at most one edge, and no edge joins a vertex to itself.
Only the connections matter; the same graph can be drawn in many ways.
A graph is _connected_ if there is a path of edges between any two of its vertices.
A _cycle_ is a closed loop of edges that does not visit any vertex twice, and its _length_ is its number of edges.
The _distance_ between two vertices is the number of edges in a shortest path between them.

<div class="annotated">

<Note>
	Vizing's theorem covers all graphs of this kind, not only cubic ones. If the largest number of edges at any vertex is Δ, the edges need either Δ or Δ + 1 colors. Graphs that need Δ are called _class 1_, and graphs that need Δ + 1 are called _class 2_.
</Note>

An edge coloring with three colors is a _<span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>_.
A cubic graph needs at least three colors, because three edges meet at each vertex.
By a theorem that Vadim Vizing proved in 1964, it never needs more than four <Cite id="vizing1964" />.

</div>

Among cubic graphs with 28 vertices and no triangle, fewer than one in a thousand need four <Cite id="brinkmann2026" />.

	No other value of _n_ below 40 has any.
</Note>

Some cubic graphs without a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> are small modifications of others.
For example, replacing a vertex of a snark with a triangle makes a larger cubic graph that also has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>.
The usual definition of a snark excludes such graphs.

</div>

<Statement id="snark" name="snark">

A _snark_ is a connected cubic graph with these properties:

- It has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>.
- Its cycles all have length at least 5.
- Removing three or fewer edges never separates it into two parts that each contain a cycle.

</Statement>

Definitions vary.
Some authors require only the first property, and that removing one edge never disconnects the graph <Cite id="campos2011" />.

The smallest snark is the Petersen graph, which has 10 vertices and 15 edges.
It can be drawn as a pentagon, a five-pointed star inside it, and five edges joining the two.

<Statement id="petersen" name="Petersen, 1898">
	The Petersen graph has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that it has one, with colors 1, 2, and 3.
Edges of the same color cannot meet, so each color appears at most twice among the five edges of the outer pentagon.
One color must therefore appear exactly once.
Call it 3.
The colors around the pentagon are then 1, 2, 1, 2, 3.

Each vertex of the pentagon has one edge leading inward, whose color is the one that its two pentagon edges do not use.
Three of the vertices are not on the pentagon edge colored 3, so the edges leading inward from them are colored 3.
Each of the five edges of the star meets one of those three edges, so no edge of the star is colored 3.
The star is a cycle of five edges, and two colors cannot alternate around a cycle of odd length.
This is a contradiction.

</Proof>

Several conjectures in graph theory, including the [5-flow conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere-zero_flow), would be proved in general if they were proved for snarks <Cite id="lukotka2024" />.

## Construction

</BlockFigure>

A Goldberg snark is made of copies of a _block_, which has eight vertices and nine edges.
The vertices are labeled _a_ through _h_, following Mohar and Vodopivec <Cite id="mohar2006" />.
Three further edges, called _links_, join each block to the next.

</div>

<div class="annotated">

<CutFigure>
	The Petersen graph. The dashed edges are those that meet _u_, _v_, or _w_.
</CutFigure>

The block comes from the Petersen graph.
Removing a path of three vertices, such as _u_, _v_, _w_, leaves seven vertices, which are _b_ through _h_.
It makes no difference which path is chosen, because a [symmetry](/g13#symmetries) of the Petersen graph takes any path of three vertices to any other.
Five edges led from the remaining vertices to _u_, _v_, and _w_.
The one from _b_ now leads to a new vertex, _a_.
The ones from _e_, _f_, _g_, and _h_ become links to the neighboring blocks.

</div>

The blocks are arranged in a ring.
The links form part of three rings: the <Term is="outer">outer ring</Term>, which passes through _e_ and _f_ in every block; the <Term is="middle">middle ring</Term>, which passes through _g_ and _h_; and the <Term is="inner">inner ring</Term>, which passes through _a_.

## Proof that Goldberg snarks need four colors

<div class="annotated">

<Note>
	The first paragraph of the proof of <Ref id="matching" /> shows a case of the _parity lemma_.
</Note>

The proof compares the colors of the outer and middle links between neighboring blocks.

<Statement id="matching">
	In any <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> of a block and its links, the outer and middle links have the same color on exactly one of the block's two sides.
</Statement>

</div>

<div class="annotated">

<ReattachedFigure>
	The last case of the proof of <Ref id="matching" />, with the [dashed edges](#cut) restored and colored. Green, blue, and gold stand for colors 1, 2, and 3.
</ReattachedFigure>

<Proof>

Consider the seven vertices _b_ through _h_.
Five edges have exactly one end among them: the outer and middle links on each side, and the edge _ab_.
Every color appears at each of the seven vertices.
An edge between two of the seven accounts for two of those appearances, and each of the five edges accounts for one.
Since seven is odd, each color appears on an odd number of the five edges.
Three odd numbers that add up to five must be 3, 1, and 1, so one color appears on three of the five edges and the other two colors appear on one each.

Suppose that the outer and middle links have the same color on both sides.
If the two sides use one color, it appears on at least four of the five edges.
If they use two colors, each appears on at least two, but only one color appears more than once.
Neither is possible.

Suppose instead that the outer and middle links have different colors on both sides.
The color that appears three times, say 1, can appear only once on each side, so it appears once on the left, once on the right, and on _ab_.
Let 2 be the other color on the left.
Since 2 appears only once, the other color on the right is 3.
Restore the vertices _u_, _v_, and _w_ [that were removed](#cut) from the Petersen graph.
Attach the links on the right to _u_, the links on the left to _w_, and the edge from _b_ to _v_.
Color the edge _uv_ with 2 and the edge _vw_ with 3.
All three colors now meet at each of _u_, _v_, and _w_, so this is a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> of the Petersen graph.
By <Ref id="petersen" />, there is none.

</Proof>

</div>

<ChainFigure blocks={2} id="chain" label="Two blocks in a row, colored. On the left, the outer and middle links are both green. Between the blocks, they are two different colors. On the right, they are both green again." links={[1, 0, 0]} name="Two linked blocks">
	A <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> of two linked blocks. The outer and middle links have the same color on the left, different colors in the middle, and the same color on the right.
</ChainFigure>

<Statement id="theorem">
	For <i>k</i> ≥ 3, the Goldberg graph <Snark n="k" /> has a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> if and only if <i>k</i> is even.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that <Snark n="k" /> has a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>.
There are _k_ places around the ring where one block is linked to the next.
By <Ref id="matching" />, the places where the outer and middle links have the same color alternate with the places where they have different colors.
This is only possible if _k_ is even.

If _k_ is even, _k_/2 copies of [the two blocks drawn above](#chain) can be joined in a ring, as the links have the same colors on the left as on the right.
The result is a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> of <Snark n="k" />.

</Proof>

## Short cycles and connectivity

<Ref id="theorem" /> shows that Goldberg snarks have the first property in <Ref id="snark" />.
The next two propositions show that they have the other two.

<Statement id="short-cycles">
	For odd <i>k</i> ≥ 5, the only cycles of <Snark n="k" /> with length less than 8 are two of length 5 and one of length 6 in each block, and the inner ring if <i>k</i> is 5 or 7.
</Statement>

<Proof>

A cycle within one block cannot use _ab_, as there is no other way back to _a_.
The other eight edges form three paths from _c_ to _d_, of lengths 2, 3, and 3, so they contain two cycles of length 5 and one of length 6.

Consider a cycle that visits two blocks or more, and suppose that there are two neighboring blocks with none of the cycle's edges between them.
It then has a first block and a last block, and it enters and leaves each of them by two different links on the same side.
Those links end at two of _a_, _e_, and _h_, or at two of _a_, _f_, and _g_.
Within a block, any two of the former are at distance 3, and so are any two of the latter.
The cycle has at least three edges in each of the two blocks, and at least two links, so its length is at least 8.

Otherwise, the cycle uses a link between every two neighboring blocks, so it has at least _k_ links.
If they are all inner links, it is the inner ring.
If none are, then no two of its links meet, so there is another edge between each link and the next, and its length is at least <span class="nowrap">2*k* ≥ 10</span>.
If some are, then it goes at least twice between _a_ and one of _e_, _f_, _g_, and _h_ within a block, which takes at least three edges each time, and its length is at least <span class="nowrap">_k_ + 6 ≥ 11</span>.

</Proof>

<Statement id="connectivity">
	For odd <i>k</i> ≥ 5, removing three or fewer edges from <Snark n="k" /> never separates it into two parts that each contain a cycle.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that it does.
Color the vertices of one part black and those of the other part white, so that at most three edges have ends of both colors.
Call these edges _mixed_.

In each block, consider the _core_: the vertices _b_ through _h_ and the eight edges that join them.
Every edge of a core is in a cycle of the core, so a core that has vertices of both colors has at least two mixed edges.
At most one core therefore does.
The cores of neighboring blocks are joined by two links, the outer and the middle.
If one core is all black and a neighboring core is all white, both links between them are mixed.

If no core has both colors, then every core is the same color: otherwise the color would change at least twice around the ring, with two mixed edges each time.
Say that they are white.
Only vertices labeled _a_ can be black, so the black cycle is the inner ring.
Then all _k_ edges _ab_ are mixed, and _k_ is more than three.

If one core has both colors, then the other <span class="nowrap">_k_ − 1</span> cores are all the same color, as there is at most one other mixed edge.
Say that they are white.
The black cycle is not the inner ring, as then the edge _ab_ would be mixed in the other <span class="nowrap">_k_ − 1 ≥ 4</span> blocks.
It is therefore within the one core, as _b_ is white in every other block.
If it has length 6, it contains _e_, _f_, _g_, and _h_, whose four links to white cores are mixed.
If it has length 5, the links from two of those vertices are mixed, as are at least two edges of the core.
There are at least four mixed edges either way.

</Proof>

Goldberg snarks are cubic and connected, since each vertex has three edges in <Ref id="goldberg-snark" /> and each block is connected and is linked to the next.
They therefore meet <Ref id="snark" />.

## Uncolored edges

If a cubic graph has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>, at least two of its edges must be left uncolored when three colors are used.
To see this, suppose that only one edge, _uv_, is uncolored.
Every vertex other than _u_ and _v_ has an edge of each color, and _u_ and _v_ are each missing one color.
The number of vertices is even, because three times as many is twice the number of edges.
Each edge of one color covers two vertices, so the number of vertices missing that color is also even.
The color missing at _u_ is therefore the color missing at _v_.
Giving that color to _uv_ produces a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>, which is a contradiction.

The smallest number of edges that must be left uncolored is called the _resistance_ of the graph.
The resistance of every Goldberg snark is 2: the edges _ab_ and _ce_ of one block can be left uncolored, and the rest can be colored.
The drawings below show three blocks colored in this way, and two blocks with every edge colored.
The links have the same colors at both ends of both drawings, so the three blocks can be joined in a ring with any number of copies of the two.

## Hamiltonian cycles

A _Hamiltonian cycle_ is a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once, and a graph that has one is called _Hamiltonian_.

<Statement id="hamiltonian">
	Goldberg snarks have no Hamiltonian cycle.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that there is a Hamiltonian cycle.
It has 8*k* edges, which is an even number, so two colors can alternate around it.
The cycle uses two of the three edges at each vertex.
The unused edges do not meet each other, so they can all be given the third color.
This is a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>, which contradicts <Ref id="theorem" />.

</Proof>

The same proof applies to every cubic graph that has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>, and so to every snark, as every cubic graph has an even number of vertices.

Every Goldberg snark has a cycle that visits all but one of its vertices, so its _circumference_, the length of its longest cycle, is <span class="nowrap">8*k* − 1</span>.
[The cycle drawn for <Snark n={5} />](/g5#longest-cycle) passes between its second and third blocks, counting clockwise from the top, only once, along their inner link.
Two more blocks can be inserted there.
The cycle visits the first in the order _a_, _b_, _d_, _f_, _e_, _c_, _g_, _h_, and the second in the order _g_, _h_, _d_, _f_, _e_, _c_, _b_, _a_.
Repeating this gives a cycle for every odd <span class="nowrap">_k_ ≥ 5</span>.

## Properties

These are properties of <Snark n="k" /> for odd <span class="nowrap">_k_ ≥ 5</span>.

</div>

A _circular nowhere-zero r-flow_ gives each edge a direction and a number from 1 to <span class="nowrap">_r_ − 1</span>, which need not be whole.
At each vertex, the numbers on the edges leading in have the same sum as the numbers on the edges leading out.
The smallest _r_ for which a graph has one is its _circular flow number_.
The 5-flow conjecture says that it is at most 5 for every connected graph that cannot be disconnected by removing one edge.
It is less than 5 for every Goldberg snark, so the conjecture holds for them.

<span id="planar">Goldberg snarks are not planar</span>: every drawing of one has edges that cross.
This follows from the four color theorem, proved in 1976, which says that the regions of any map can be colored with four colors so that regions sharing a border have different colors.
Peter Tait showed in 1880 that the theorem is equivalent to the statement that every planar cubic graph has a <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>, provided that removing one edge never disconnects the graph <Cite id="tait1880" />.
If removing one edge disconnected a cubic graph, both parts would contain a cycle.
By the third property in <Ref id="snark" />, snarks therefore satisfy that condition, but they have no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span>.

## History

Peter Tait showed in 1880 that the four color problem is equivalent to one about [edge colorings of planar cubic graphs](#properties) <Cite id="tait1880" />.
Julius Petersen showed in 1898 that the Petersen graph has no <span class="nowrap">3-edge-coloring</span> <Cite id="petersen1898" />.
It was the only known snark until 1946, when Danilo Blanuša found another <Cite id="blanusa1946" />.
In 1975, Rufus Isaacs described two infinite families of snarks, including the flower snarks <Cite id="isaacs1975" />.
Martin Gardner gave snarks their name in 1976, after Lewis Carroll's poem _The Hunting of the Snark_ <Cite id="gardner1976" />.
In the same year, Isaacs described a construction by F. Loupekine that also [joins blocks in a ring](#related-graphs) <Cite id="berman2017" />.

Mark K. Goldberg published the Goldberg snarks in 1981 <Cite id="goldberg1981" />.
Later papers studied their embeddings in surfaces <Cite id="mohar2006" />, their circular chromatic index <Cite id="ghebleh2007" />, and their total chromatic number <Cite id="campos2011" />.
[Goldberg](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/~goldberg/) received a doctorate from the Institute of Mathematics in Novosibirsk, and was later a professor of computer science at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute.
The Goldberg–Seymour conjecture, on edge colorings of graphs in which two vertices may be joined by more than one edge, is named after Goldberg and Paul Seymour.
A proof was published in 2025 <Cite id="chen2025" />.
