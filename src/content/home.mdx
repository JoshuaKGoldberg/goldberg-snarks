## Edge coloring

A _graph_ is a set of points, called _vertices_, together with a set of lines, called _edges_, that each join two vertices.
Two vertices are joined by at most one edge.
Only the connections matter; the same graph can be drawn in many ways.
A graph is _cubic_ if every vertex has exactly three edges.

<Annotated>

<Note>
	Vizing's theorem covers all graphs in which two vertices are joined by at most one edge. If the largest number of edges at any vertex is Δ, the edges need either Δ or Δ + 1 colors. Graphs that need Δ are called _class 1_, and graphs that need Δ + 1 are called _class 2_.
</Note>

An _edge coloring_ gives each edge a color so that edges sharing a vertex have different colors.
If it uses three colors, it is a _3-edge-coloring_.
A cubic graph needs at least three colors, because three edges meet at each vertex.
By a theorem that Vadim Vizing proved in 1964, it never needs more than four.

</Annotated>

Most cubic graphs have a 3-edge-coloring.
Among those with 28 vertices and no cycle shorter than four edges, fewer than one in a thousand do not <Cite id="brinkmann2026" />.

A snark is a cubic graph that has no 3-edge-coloring.
Some graphs fit that description only because of a small feature, such as a triangle, that can be removed without changing whether a 3-edge-coloring exists.
The usual definition excludes them.

</Annotated>

<Statement id="snark" kind="Definition" name="snark" number={1}>
	A _snark_ is a connected cubic graph that has no 3-edge-coloring, has no cycle of fewer than five edges, and cannot be separated into two parts that each contain a cycle by removing fewer than four edges.
</Statement>

Definitions vary.
Martin Gardner, who introduced the name, required only that removing one edge never disconnects the graph <Cite id="brinkmann2026" />.

The smallest snark is the Petersen graph, which has 10 vertices and 15 edges.

<Statement id="petersen" kind="Lemma" name="Petersen, 1898" number={2}>
	The Petersen graph has no 3-edge-coloring.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that it has one, with colors 1, 2, and 3.
Edges of the same color cannot touch, so each color appears at most twice among the five edges of the outer pentagon.
One color must therefore appear exactly once.
Call it 3.
The colors around the pentagon are then 1, 2, 1, 2, 3.

Each vertex of the pentagon has one edge leading inward, whose color is the one that its two pentagon edges do not use.
Three of the vertices do not touch the pentagon edge colored 3, so the edges leading inward from them are colored 3.
Each of the five edges of the inner star shares a vertex with one of those three edges, so no edge of the star is colored 3.
The star is a cycle of five edges, and two colors cannot alternate around a cycle of odd length.
This is a contradiction.

</Proof>

Several open problems in graph theory, including the cycle double cover conjecture and the 5-flow conjecture, would be settled for all graphs if they were settled for snarks <Cite id="chladny2010" />.

A Goldberg snark is made of copies of a _block_, which has eight vertices and nine edges.
The vertices are labeled _a_ through _h_, following Mohar and Vodopivec <Cite id="mohar2006" />.
Three further edges, called _links_, join each block to the next.

</Annotated>

<Annotated>

<CutFigure>

</CutFigure>

The block comes from the Petersen graph.
Removing three consecutive vertices from the Petersen graph leaves seven vertices, which are _b_ through _h_.
It makes no difference which three are chosen, because the Petersen graph is symmetric.
The vertex _a_ is then added and joined to _b_.

</Annotated>

The blocks are arranged in a ring, and each is joined to the next by three links.
The links form part of three rings: the <Term is="outer">outer ring</Term>, which passes through _e_ and _f_ in every block; the <Term is="middle">middle ring</Term>, which passes through _g_ and _h_; and the <Term is="inner">inner ring</Term>, which passes through _a_.

## Proof that three colors are not enough

<Annotated>

<Note>
	The counting argument in the first paragraph of this proof is called the _parity lemma_.
</Note>

The proof looks at the links between neighboring blocks, and at whether the <Term is="outer">outer</Term> and <Term is="middle">middle</Term> links there have the same color.

<Statement id="matching" kind="Lemma" number={4}>
	In any 3-edge-coloring of a block and its links, the outer and middle links have the same color on exactly one of the block's two sides.
</Statement>

</Annotated>

<Annotated>

<ReattachedFigure>
	The last case of the proof of Lemma 4. The dashed edges of Figure 4 have been restored and colored.
</ReattachedFigure>

<Proof>

Remove _a_, and consider the seven vertices _b_ through _h_.
Five edges have exactly one end among them: the outer and middle links on each side, and the edge _ab_.
Every color appears at each of the seven vertices.
An edge between two of the seven accounts for two of them, and each of the five edges accounts for one.
Since seven is odd, each color appears on an odd number of the five edges.
Three odd numbers that add up to five must be 3, 1, and 1, so one color appears on three of the five edges and the other two colors appear on one each.

Suppose that the outer and middle links have the same color on both sides.
If the two sides use one color, it appears on at least four of the five edges.
If they use two colors, both appear on at least two.
Neither is possible.

Suppose instead that the outer and middle links have different colors on both sides.
The color that appears three times, say 1, can appear only once on each side, so it appears once on the left, once on the right, and on _ab_.
Let 2 be the other color on the left.
The other color on the right is then 3.
Restore the vertices _u_, _v_, and _w_ [that were removed](#cut) from the Petersen graph.
Attach the links on the right to _u_, the links on the left to _w_, and the edge from _b_ to _v_.
Color the edge _uv_ with 2 and the edge _vw_ with 3.
All three colors now meet at each of _u_, _v_, and _w_, so this is a 3-edge-coloring of the Petersen graph.
By [Lemma 2](#petersen), there is none.

</Proof>

</Annotated>

<ChainFigure>
	A 3-edge-coloring of two linked blocks. The outer and middle links have the same color on the left, different colors in the middle, and the same color on the right.
</ChainFigure>

<Statement id="theorem" kind="Theorem" number={5}>
	For <i>k</i> ≥ 3, the Goldberg graph <Snark n="k" /> has a 3-edge-coloring if and only if <i>k</i> is even.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that <Snark n="k" /> has a 3-edge-coloring.
There are _k_ places around the ring where one block is linked to the next.
By Lemma 4, the places where the outer and middle links have the same color alternate with the places where they have different colors.
This is only possible if _k_ is even.

If _k_ is even, take _k_/2 copies of [the two blocks in Figure 7](#chain) and join them in a ring.
The links have the same colors on the left as on the right, so the result is a 3-edge-coloring.

</Proof>

### Uncolored edges

At least two edges of a snark must be left uncolored when three colors are used.
To see this, suppose that only one edge, _uv_, is uncolored.
Every vertex other than _u_ and _v_ has an edge of each color, and _u_ and _v_ are each missing one color.
The edges of one color cover two vertices each, and the number of vertices is even, so the number of vertices missing that color is also even.
The color missing at _u_ is therefore the color missing at _v_.
Giving that color to _uv_ produces a 3-edge-coloring, which a snark does not have.

The smallest number of edges that must be left uncolored is called the _resistance_ of the graph.
The resistance of every Goldberg snark is 2: the edges _ab_ and _ce_ of one block can be left uncolored, and the rest can be colored.

### Hamiltonian cycles

A _Hamiltonian cycle_ is a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once.

<Statement id="hamiltonian" kind="Corollary" number={6}>
	Goldberg snarks have no Hamiltonian cycle.
</Statement>

<Proof>

Suppose that there is a Hamiltonian cycle.
It has 8*k* edges, which is an even number, so two colors can alternate around it.
The cycle uses two of the three edges at each vertex.
The unused edges do not touch each other, so they can all be given the third color.
This is a 3-edge-coloring, which contradicts Theorem 5.

</Proof>

The same proof applies to every snark.
For odd _k_ from 5 to 15, <Snark n="k" /> has a cycle that visits all but one of its vertices.

## History

Julius Petersen showed in 1898 that the Petersen graph has no 3-edge-coloring.
It was the only known snark until 1946, when Danilo Blanuša found another.
In 1975, Rufus Isaacs described the first infinite families of snarks, including the flower snarks <Cite id="isaacs1975" />.
Martin Gardner gave snarks their name in 1976, after Lewis Carroll's poem _The Hunting of the Snark_ <Cite id="gardner1976" />.

Mark K. Goldberg published the Goldberg snarks in 1981 <Cite id="goldberg1981" />.
[Goldberg](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/~goldberg) received a doctorate from the Institute of Mathematics in Novosibirsk, and was later a professor of computer science at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute.
The Goldberg–Seymour conjecture, on edge colorings of graphs in which two vertices may be joined by more than one edge, is named after Goldberg and Paul Seymour.
A proof was published in 2025 <Cite id="chen2025" />.
